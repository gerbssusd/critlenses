<p class="kicker">Definition</p>

# What Is a Critical Lens?

A set of assumptions and questions that decides **what counts as evidence** in a literary argument (Tyson; Barry).

<div class="claim mt-6 text-base">
No lens is "correct"; each brings some features of a text into focus and leaves others out (Bertens).
</div>

::right::

<div class="h-full grid place-items-center">
  <LensPrism />
</div>

<!--
So what exactly is a critical lens? Lois Tyson and Peter Barry both describe a theory of literature as a set of assumptions that decides what counts as evidence in an argument. Think of light passing through a prism. The text stays the same, but each lens separates out a different band of meaning. As Hans Bertens reminds us, no lens is correct. Each one brings certain features into focus, and leaves others in shadow.
-->

# Form & Structure

  <LensCard name="Structuralism & Semiotics" era="1950s–1970s" color="var(--c-teal)"

<p class="caption">Right: Ferdinand de Saussure, photograph by F. Jullien · Public domain</p>

<!--
Formalism, often called New Criticism, treats the poem or story as a self-contained object. Cleanth Brooks and his colleagues practiced close reading, tracing how irony, paradox, and tension resolve into a unified whole. Biography and history are deliberately set aside. Structuralism widens the frame. Building on Ferdinand de Saussure's account of language as a system of signs, structuralists look for the codes and binary oppositions, such as nature and culture, or light and dark, that let any text make meaning at all.
-->

Deconstruction, associated with Jacques Derrida, takes those binaries and pushes on them until they give way. Because language is unstable, a text always says more, and less, than it intends. The critic looks for the place where the text contradicts itself. Reader-response criticism shifts attention to us. Stanley Fish argues that meaning is produced by readers working within interpretive communities. Here we ask how a text guides our expectations, frustrates them, and changes from one era of readers to the next.
-->

<!--
Psychoanalytic criticism draws on Sigmund Freud's model of the psyche: repression, dreams, and the struggle among id, ego, and superego. Critics look for what characters avoid, and for symbols and slips that reveal hidden desires. Archetypal criticism looks outward to what cultures share. Influenced by Carl Jung and systematized by Northrop Frye, it traces recurring patterns such as the quest, the scapegoat, and the cycle of death and rebirth, and asks how a work echoes or revises older myths.
-->

<!--
Three lenses read literature through history and society.

[click] Historical and biographical criticism, the oldest of these approaches, treats the author's life and moment as keys to a text's allusions and meanings.

[click] New Historicism, led by Stephen Greenblatt, reads literature as one document among many, alongside laws, sermons, and letters, that circulate power in a culture. It asks whose voices a text amplifies and whose it silences.

[click] And Marxist criticism, as Terry Eagleton explains, examines class, labor, money, and ideology, asking whether a work supports or exposes the economic order that produced it.
-->

Critical race theory, introduced in Richard Delgado and Jean Stefancic's widely used primer, treats race as a social construction and racism as something built into institutions, laws, and stories. It values counter-narratives from people of color. Postcolonial criticism, inaugurated in large part by Edward Said's Orientalism, studies empire and its aftermath: how colonizers represent the so-called Other, and how colonized peoples write back in their own voices.
-->

<p class="kicker">A Conversation Across a Century</p>

# When the Lenses Emerged

<LensTimeline class="mt-2" />

<!--
Seen on a timeline, these lenses form a conversation across more than a century. Biographical criticism and Freud's psychoanalysis come first. New Criticism and archetypal criticism rise in the mid-twentieth century. Structuralism gives way to deconstruction and reader-response in the nineteen sixties. Feminist, postcolonial, and critical race lenses follow, and the nineteen nineties bring gender studies, queer theory, ecocriticism, and disability studies. Newer lenses rarely replace older ones; they argue with them.
-->

A reader using a different lens could disagree, and that is exactly what makes it worth writing.
